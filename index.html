Overview

Ten bonds, one curve, three ways of finding it.

A bond’s price is the sum of its discounted cash flows. Turn that sentence around and it becomes a question: given the prices, what were the discount factors? This is the answer, worked three separate ways so that each one checks the others.

What the project does

The input is a set of ten coupon-bearing government bonds, one maturing each year out to ten years. Each is described by three numbers: how long until it matures, what it costs today, and what coupon it pays. Nothing else.

From that, a YieldCurve class recovers the discount-factor curve — the price today of one unit of currency delivered at each future date — and from those factors derives the three rate curves that practitioners actually quote: spot rates, yields to maturity, and forward rates.

The interesting part is not the answer but the fact that it can be reached three different ways. A direct linear solve, a numerical optimiser and a hand-rolled recursion have almost nothing in common as procedures, yet they agree to four decimals. When independent methods converge, the result is probably not an artefact of the method.

The check that matters

Multiplying the cash-flow matrix back through the recovered discount factors reproduces all ten market prices exactly. A curve that cannot reprice the instruments it was built from is not a curve.
